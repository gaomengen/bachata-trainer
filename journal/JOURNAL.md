## 2026-10-20 · Full weight transfer, and a private lesson on leading

**Source:** Bachata 1, then the performance series at Level Up (Tuesday)

### Bachata 1
- Always good to revisit the basics and weight transfer.
- **Biggest takeaway: weight transfer has to be "fully committed"** (my word). When
  you make a move, make sure the weight has fully transferred, not split between your
  feet. If it's split, the next move needs an extra adjustment to finish the
  transfer first, because you can't move a foot that still carries part of your
  weight.
- Practised **Madrid steps**. Very different from the versions I learned at Meraki
  in Louisville, but I got them. Different variations.

### Performance series: just me and the instructor
The other student didn't show, so the instructor offered to teach me more about
leading.
- **Lead even in choreography.** The follow knows what's next, but that doesn't mean
  the lead gives no communication. Step, prep the follow, then make the move.
- **Three connection points: the middle two fingers, the elbow, the shoulder.**
  Crucial for communication between lead and follow.
- **Shoulder-nudge left turn:** extend the left hand on 5; to send the follow into a
  full turn to their left, let the hand go and use the shoulder, "boomerang style":
  a slight bump on the follow's right shoulder from behind, nudge forward, then back.
  That signals a turn, and they bounce into it. Then we meet again in the basic.
  (The alternative is turning with hands connected, but that's not this move.)
- **Checking tension:** when the connection or tension isn't what you expect, say
  so: "Do you feel me? I don't feel you enough." Show it with the first knuckles of
  the middle two fingers, pulling from the hand, not the arm: "pull me like this".
  They adjust their tension.
- **Hands move with your body.** When I moved left and right, my hands stayed in the
  same place, left behind my torso. Moving the hands with the body sends a clear
  message: we're doing a basic.
- **A strong signal isn't rude or strong-arming.** Follows expect a clear, strong
  signal; it's easier to follow. Biggest takeaway: **I have the freedom to apply
  strength**, as long as it doesn't hurt them.

### Why it works
- **Full transfer** is the end of the hip-delay chain (foot, torso, hip): the
  transfer is only done when the hip fully settles over the foot. Split weight means
  a hidden extra step, which makes you late and makes the follow feel a stutter.
- **Fingers, elbow, shoulder:** when all three are connected, the arm carries the
  signal from the body to the follow; a loose point swallows it.
- **Hands with the body** completes "torso first": the frame is torso **plus hands**,
  moving as one unit.
- **Strong vs shove:** firm, through the body, smooth = clear. Sudden, from the arm,
  yanking = a shove. That fits "lead from the body, not the arms": strength is fine
  when it comes from the body through a connected frame, scaled to each partner.

**Try next**
- Basic in the mirror: check that the hands travel with the torso.
- Drill the shoulder-nudge left turn in shadow practice: prep, release on 5, nudge,
  meet in the basic.
- At every step, check that the weight is fully over one foot before the next.

## 2026-10-15 · Footwork at home, sensual at the socials

**Source:** my own reflection after the Wednesday social

**What I realized**
- Everything I've practised from TikTok and other videos is **footwork**, which is
  really **traditional (Dominican) bachata**. I love it: it looks cool, and you can do
  it on your own.
- The classes I'm taking now are **sensual bachata**: lots of turns and patterns, on
  the shoulder, behind the head, double turns.
- I want to be good at both. But I think I'll be happier dancing traditional: the
  energy feels different, and it's more about your own expression through footwork.
  Sensual feels more about connection with a partner (traditional can do that too).
- The mismatch: I practise footwork at home, but the socials are sensual. Even the
  music was different: remixed pop with a bachata touch.

**Why it matters**
- **Dominican (traditional) bachata:** footwork-heavy, grounded, playful, with lots of
  solo expression inside the partnership. Music is traditional bachata: requinto
  guitar, bongo, güira, bass, with the derecho/majao/mambo structure.
- **Sensual bachata:** developed in Spain; body waves, turns, dips, head movement and a
  close connection. Music is often slower **remixes of pop and R&B songs**. These have
  pop structure (verse, chorus, big breaks) more than majao/mambo sections.
- So the musicality cues I've been learning (the güira, the mambo) fit traditional
  songs best; on remixes, listen for breaks, drops and builds instead.
- **Footwork isn't wasted at a sensual social.** It builds grounding, timing and
  texture, and the basic is shared. It just won't stand in for partner patterns.

**Plan**
- For the rest of the LA month, put **social practice time into sensual patterns**,
  since that's what the socials dance (the vocabulary list).
- Keep footwork as my joy and warm-up, and use it as texture in the basic.
- Ask around for Dominican or traditional bachata nights in LA.

## 2026-10-13 · Eyes-closed trust drill, and the leader's responsibility

**Source:** class (last night). A teaching method I want to keep in case I teach
someday.

**The drill**
- Lead and follow face each other; all the pairs form a circle.
- The follow puts their hands on the lead's chest, firm, so the two make a solid
  connection. The follow **closes their eyes**.
- The lead watches the instructor's hand signals: **forward, back, hold**. Wave
  forward and the lead moves forward; wave back and they move back.
- It teaches a lot about how partners work together, and about trust.

**What stuck with me**
- The leader is the one who invites the follow to dance, so the leader is responsible
  for them: not only their movement but their **safety**. Never grab their hand.
  Never lead them into other people or a collision. Make sure they're safe with you.

**Why it works**
- With eyes closed, the follow can only use the connection; they can't watch and
  guess. That's why it builds trust fast.
- The contact is on the chest, so the leader has to lead with their body, not their
  arms (same as the diagonal box lead).
- The signals come without warning, so the leader has to react and commit cleanly:
  forward, back or hold, nothing half-way.
- The circle puts pairs close together, so the leader has to watch the floor, which is
  the safety point in practice.

**Variations if I teach it**
- Follow's eyes open first, then closed.
- Add side steps, then the basic with a tap.
- Put music on: move on the beat, not just on signals.
- Swap roles, so leaders feel what being led blind is like.
- Ask follows afterwards which leads felt clear and which felt like pushing.

## 2026-10-11 · Some patterns are only done on one side

**Source:** my own observation from classes

**What I noticed**
- When I started, I assumed that if you learn a move on one side (a hammerlock, a right
  or left turn), you have to learn it on the other side too.
- But most people don't. Some patterns are commonly done on one side only. The
  hammerlock, maybe locking on the follow's right side?
- In class I expected the instructor to tell us to practise the other side too, but it
  never came. So maybe some patterns are just one side, and that's it.

**Why it works**
- **The hold isn't symmetrical:** leader's left hand holds the follow's right hand,
  leader's right arm on the follow's back. Patterns start from that hold, so the
  mirror needs a different hand connection and a different way in; it's really a
  separate pattern.
- **Social dancing runs on shared habits.** Everyone learns the standard version, so a
  follow recognises it from the first touch, even with a stranger. A mirrored version
  is harder to lead clearly.
- **Patterns join up:** the standard version ends somewhere that leads easily into the
  next common move.
- **Class time:** better to nail the version you'll actually use.
- **What does go both ways:** the basics, **turns** (right and left are both common),
  and my own body: practise turns and styling on both sides to stay balanced.

**Try next**
- Ask in class: which side is the hammerlock usually done on, and why?
- Practise solo turns in both directions.

## 2026-10-10 · Texture, and my "syncopation reserve"

**Source:** Chelsea's intermediate class, plus hours of listening to bachata over the
last two days

**What I learned**
- **Texture:** instead of hitting every step of the basic evenly, vary them. Like
  "dun dun toe", or sometimes two steps in one beat. It really adds texture.
- From listening a lot, I realized how important **syncopation** is in bachata music.
  Connecting the two: almost every time there's texture in the steps, there's
  syncopation in the music.
- My own term: a **"syncopation reserve"**. If I understand music well enough, even an
  unfamiliar song, I can feel the build-up to a syncopation, and in that moment I pull
  something from my reserve and move my steps differently. That syncs steps and music
  much better than a bland basic.
- Not sure it's 100% correct; this is my understanding right now.

**Why it works (and the refinement)**
- Texture matches the music: dancers change their footwork when the music changes.
  Bachata is full of syncopation (bongo, guitar), so it's often the trigger.
- **Predicting it in unfamiliar songs** works because bachata is built in phrases of
  eight counts, usually four eights at a time. Fills and builds tend to come at the
  end of a phrase, just before something new starts. Listening a lot trains this.
- **Refinement:** syncopation is the most common trigger, not the only one. Texture
  follows **musical accents** in general:
  - **breaks and stops:** the music drops out, so pause or hold;
  - **section changes:** into the **mambo** (high-energy instrumental section, busy
    guitar, the bongo switches to cowbell), so steps get bigger or busier;
  - **hits on the beat:** a strong guitar or vocal accent can deserve a sharper step.
- Dancers usually call the reserve **vocabulary**. Two rules keep it working:
  1. **Drill it until it's automatic.** There's no time to think in the moment ("no
     fast track").
  2. **Contrast.** Plain basics are the background; texture stands out because it's
     saved for moments the music marks. Texture on every count cancels itself out.

**Try next**
- Pick 2–3 textures (double step, "dun dun toe", a hold) and drill each until automatic.
- Play a song I don't know and call out the end of each phrase before it arrives.
- Listen for breaks and the move into the mambo, and use a texture there.

## 2026-10-08 · The most common beginner mistake: missing the tap

**Source:** my own observation

**What I noticed**
- One of the easiest mistakes beginners make is missing a count, usually the tap.

**Why it happens**
- **The tap carries no weight.** Counts 1, 2 and 3 each move the weight, so the body
  feels them as steps. The tap doesn't, so the body skips it and goes straight into
  the next 1. The 4-count becomes a 3-count and drifts off the music.
- **The rush.** After three steps the body wants to keep walking; holding a beat where
  nothing moves feels unnatural at first.
- **The opposite mistake:** stepping *down* on the tap. Then the wrong foot is free
  and the next basic starts on the wrong side. The tap is a touch, not a step.

**Fixes**
- Give the tap a job: it's when the hip settles and pops (see the hip delay entries),
  so the beat feels full, not empty.
- Count "1, 2, 3, **tap**" out loud instead of "4".
- Use the "and": "3, 4 &, 1". The "&" holds me in place so I don't start 1 early.

## 2026-10-08 · Drive from the standing leg, and why torso-first keeps me upright

**Source:** practice (trying out the hip delay)

**What I realized**
- If I leave my centre of weight (hip) behind when I move, I get **thrust**. Moving
  left with the hip still back, I get more drive from my right leg.
- Moving the torso and foot slightly ahead of the hip **doesn't contradict** staying
  straight and upright. When I tried it, it actually **helped** me stay upright.

**Why it works**
- **Drive:** a leg can only push against the floor while it's carrying weight. With
  the hip still over the right leg, that leg is loaded and pushes me left. If the hip
  went first, the right leg would be empty and I'd be falling into the step instead
  of pushing into it. The hip delay looks good *and* powers the step.
- **Uprightness:** if the hip led, the upper body would get left behind and tilt away,
  bending me into a "C". With the torso moving along with the foot and the hip
  settling in underneath, everything ends up stacked: shoulders over hip over foot.

**Try next**
- Feel the push from the standing leg on every step of the side basic.
- Compare in the mirror: hip-first versus torso-first, and watch the shoulders.

## 2026-10-08 · What is "your weight"? The hip delay

**Source:** TikTok video on hip movement, plus my own thinking

**What I learned**
- The video says the hip moves *delayed*: the torso moves toward where you're going
  first, and the hip follows slightly behind.
- That made me ask what "weight" means in "weight transfer". Torso? Hip? Leg? Whole
  body?
- My answer: in bachata, your weight is your **hip**. Side basic left: the leg and the
  torso go first; they're the instruments that move the weight. Once they're in
  position, the weight, meaning the hip, transfers onto the left foot.
- This feels so right, but I wasn't sure it's actually right.

**Why it works**
- In physics, your weight acts at your **centre of mass**, which for a standing person
  sits low in the pelvis, just below the navel. So "weight = hip" is close to
  literally true. **Weight transfer = moving the pelvis from over one foot to over the
  other.**
- The sequence: (1) the foot reaches, no weight; (2) the torso starts moving, which is
  the lead my partner feels; (3) the hip arrives last and settles over the foot, which
  is the actual transfer.
- The "delay" is the gap between torso and hip, and that lag is what makes the hip
  motion visible. As the hip settles, that leg straightens a little and the hip tilts
  out on its own (same as the knee point from 2026-10-07).
- **Watch out:** "torso first" doesn't mean leaning. The torso travels as an upright
  unit, sliding a few centimetres, with no bending at the waist. Leaning puts the
  weight ahead of the foot and costs balance.

**Try next**
- Slow test: stop with the torso over the left foot but the hip not yet there. If I'm
  still balanced on the right leg, the delay is under control.
- Check in the mirror that the shoulders stay level while the torso moves.

## 2026-10-07 · Counting the "and"

**Source:** class (how people count)

**What I learned**
- Normal counting is 1, 2, 3, 4. Some people add "and": "1 and 2, 3, 4", or
  "1, 2, 3 and 4".
- Adding the "and" makes my steps easier. I don't have to think about it; if I follow
  the "and", the next move comes naturally and becomes muscle memory.
- My understanding: the "and" is like a syncopated note.

**Why it works**
- The **"and" is the half-beat between numbers** (1 & 2 & 3 & 4). Counting it on every
  beat doesn't add steps; it fills the gaps so I feel the time in between
  (**subdivision**).
- The "and" is where I **prepare**: weight shifts, knee softens, foot starts moving.
  Then the step lands right on the number. It also stops me rushing, especially the
  tap.
- In the music, that in-between pulse is mostly the **güira** (the metal scraper,
  "ch-ch-ch").
- **Syncopation** is different: an actual step on an "and" that normally has none.
  The classic one replaces the tap with two quick steps: **"3 & 4"** = step, step,
  step instead of step, tap. "1, 2, 3 and 4" is that shape.
- Subdivision is the timing tool; syncopation is the move. The first makes the
  second easy.

**Try next**
- Count "1 & 2 & 3 & 4" out loud through a whole song with the basic, no extra steps.
- Then try the "3 & 4" syncopation in place of the tap.
- Listen for the güira and notice the "and" without counting.

## 2026-10-07 · Stepping technique: soft knees, foot first, hip follows

**Source:** class (things instructors have said)

**What I heard**
- Don't stomp the ground when you step.
- Keep your knees bent and your upper body straight.
- "Lead with your foot and your hip follows." Something about leading with the head.
- I haven't figured out yet how these fit together.

**Why it works**
- **Soft knees** absorb each step, so you land quietly instead of stomping, and keep
  your height level (no bobbing).
- **A quiet, tall upper body** is what your partner connects to.
- **The order of a step:** (1) the foot reaches and lands lightly on the ball of the
  foot, with no weight yet; (2) then your weight and hips move over it; (3) the hip
  movement comes from the knees: the weighted leg straightens a little and the other
  softens, which tilts the hip by itself. Don't push the hip.
- **"Lead with the head" probably means posture.** The head stays level and over the
  body; throwing it forward pulls you off balance. Partner leading comes from the
  centre or chest (same idea as the diagonal box lead).

**Try next**
- Slow side basic: place the foot with no weight, pause, then move the weight over it
  and feel which knee straightens. The hip should move without being pushed.
- Ask my instructor what they mean by "lead with your head".

## 2026-10-07 · Diagonal lead into the box step

**Source:** class

**What I learned**
For the box step, instead of starting with a plain side basic to the left, the leader
can step **diagonally forward-left** on count 1 and finish the box from there. Two
ways to use it:
1. **Diagonal entry:** diagonal on the first step only, then the regular box. Once
   the follow has read it, there's no need to keep signalling.
2. **All diagonal:** keep the whole box on the diagonals, including going diagonally
   back.

**Why it works**
- With a plain side basic, counts 1–4 of a box feel the same as any side basic, so the
  follow only finds out it's a box on count 5.
- The diagonal step moves the leader's frame toward the follow. They feel themselves
  sent diagonally back on count 1, so they know a box is coming straight away.
- The lead comes from the body (chest and frame), not the arms. If the arms push, it
  feels like a shove.
- Bonus: cutting the corner makes the box smaller, which helps on a crowded floor.
