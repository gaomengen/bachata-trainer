Leading

The leader's job: commit, and keep them safe

What I've learned about leading so far, from a blindfold drill, a private lesson, and one comment that stung.

From my bachata learning journal · October 20, 2026

"The men just won't lead"

Back in Denver, during a conversation about leading and following, a woman burst out: "The men just won't lead." It bothered me for weeks. Maybe it's true, but we're learning too. Leading takes practice, and a lot of beginner leads are hesitant simply because they're new to the dance.

Later I understood what she was probably frustrated about. A follow can't fix a dance on their own. When the lead is unsure, they get mixed signals and are left guessing, and that feels worse than a simple dance. So "won't lead" probably meant won't commit, not "can't do fancy moves." And committing is something a beginner can do from day one.

The leader is responsible

One idea from class stuck with me more than any pattern: you're the one who invited your partner to dance, so you're responsible for them. Not only their movement, but their safety. Don't grab or yank. Never lead them into another couple. Watch the floor behind your follow, because they're the one moving backwards.

A drill that teaches trust

This is the best teaching exercise I've seen so far, and I'd use it if I ever teach:

  • Partners face each other, and all the couples form a circle.
  • The follow puts their hands flat on the lead's chest, firmly, and closes their eyes.
  • The lead watches the instructor's hand signals: forward, back, hold.

With their eyes closed, the follow can only use the connection. Because the contact is on the chest, the lead can't steer with their arms and has to move with their whole body. The signals come without warning, so the lead has to commit to each one. And because the couples are close together, the lead has to watch the floor.

Variations if you teach it: start with eyes open, add side steps and then the basic, put music on, swap roles, and ask the follows afterwards which leads felt clear.

What a private lesson taught me

One night the other student in my performance class didn't show up, so the instructor spent the hour on leading with me.

  • Lead even when they know what's coming. In choreography the follow knows the next move, but you still prep and signal. That's how you stay together, and it builds the habit you need at socials.
  • Three connection points: fingers, elbow, shoulder. When all three are connected, your arm carries the signal from your body to your partner. If one of them goes loose, the signal gets lost there.
  • Your hands move with your body. I found out I was leaving my hands behind when I moved side to side. The frame is your torso and your hands moving together. When they travel with you, even a plain basic is a clear signal.
  • A strong signal isn't rude. Follows expect a clear, firm signal; it's easier to follow. The difference is where it comes from. Firm, smooth and coming from your body is clear. Sudden and yanked from the arm is a shove.
  • Check the connection out loud. If the tension doesn't feel right, ask: "Do you feel me?" Show the pull you mean with your fingers, from the hand and not the arm.

The biggest takeaway for me was permission. I'm allowed to lead with real intention, as long as it never hurts. A confident, simple lead beats a hesitant fancy one. And going back to the basic isn't a failure. It's a decision, and your partner can feel that you're making it.
