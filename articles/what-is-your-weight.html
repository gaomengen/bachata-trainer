What is your weight?

Every teacher says "transfer your weight." Nobody told me what my weight actually was. Once I worked it out, my basic changed.

From my bachata learning journal · October 8, 2026

Every bachata class I've taken has said some version of "transfer your weight." I nodded along for months. Then one day I watched a short video about hip movement and it hit me: I didn't know what my weight was. My torso? My hips? My legs? My whole body?

Here's where I've landed, and it's made my basic feel completely different.

Your weight is your hip

In physics, your weight acts through one point: your centre of mass. For a standing person that point sits low in the pelvis, just below the belly button. So when a teacher says "transfer your weight," what they really mean is: move your pelvis from over one foot to over the other.

That sounds obvious written down. It wasn't obvious to me on the dance floor.

Foot, then torso, then hip

Take a side basic to the left. The order goes like this:

  1. The foot reaches. The left foot lands lightly, on the ball of the foot, with no weight on it yet.
  2. The torso starts to travel. It moves toward the left foot, staying upright. With a partner, this is the lead. It's what they feel.
  3. The hip arrives last. It settles over the left foot. That's the actual weight transfer.

The video called it a "hip delay": the torso goes first and the hip follows a little behind. That small lag is what makes bachata hip movement visible. You don't push the hip out. As your weight lands, that leg straightens slightly, the other knee softens, and the hip tilts on its own.

The delay gives you drive

When I tried this, I noticed something I didn't expect. Leaving my hip behind for a moment gave me push.

It makes sense once you think about it. A leg can only push against the floor while it's carrying weight. If I'm moving left and my hip is still over my right leg, that right leg is loaded and can drive me into the step. If my hip shoots over first, the right leg has nothing to push with, and I end up falling into the step instead of pushing into it.

It keeps you upright, too

I was worried "torso first" would fight everything I'd been told about staying tall and straight. It does the opposite. If the hip leads, the upper body gets left behind and you bend into a C shape. When the torso moves with the foot and the hip settles in underneath, you finish stacked: shoulders over hip over foot.

The one rule: the torso travels as a unit. It slides a few centimetres. It doesn't lean or tip at the waist.

Finish the transfer

A few weeks later a teacher added the last piece, which I now call being "fully committed." Get your weight all the way onto the new foot, not split between your feet. If it's split, the next move needs a hidden extra step to finish the transfer first. You end up late, and your partner feels the stutter.

A test you can try

Do a side basic very slowly. Stop when your torso is over the left foot but your hip hasn't arrived yet. If you're still balanced on your right leg, you've got the delay under control. Then let the hip settle and feel which knee straightens.

Soft knees, quiet upper body. The foot reaches, the torso follows, the hip arrives last. That's the whole step.
