The basic steps

Every one of these is three steps and a tap, over a count of 4. The tap is the signature of bachata — that's where the hip pops. Pick a step and watch which foot lights up.

What you're doing, in words

1 · Side basic

The one you'll use most. Also called the side-to-side or lateral basic.

  1. 1 — step to the left with your left foot
  2. 2 — bring your right foot to meet it, take the weight
  3. 3 — step left again with your left foot
  4. 4 — tap your right toe beside your left, and pop the hip
  5. 5–8 — the same thing mirrored, travelling right

2 · Forward & back basic

Same rhythm, different direction. This is the one you'll dance in closed position with a partner.

  1. 1 — step forward with your left
  2. 2 — right foot steps in place
  3. 3 — left foot comes back to centre
  4. 4 — tap right, hip pops
  5. 5–8 — mirrored, stepping back with the right

3 · Box step

The two basics joined into a square. Good for learning to change direction without losing the count.

  1. 1–4 — a side basic to the left, ending on the tap
  2. 5–8 — a forward-and-back basic, ending on the tap

Teachers vary on the box — some travel forward on 1–4 and sideways on 5–8, some square it off differently. Any version that keeps three steps and a tap per four counts is doing the job.

The three things beginners get wrong:
1. Skipping the tap on 4. Without it you're just walking.
2. Stepping too big. Bachata steps are small — a shoe's length.
3. Rushing. The tap is a whole beat, not a hurried afterthought.

The styles

Same music, same count of four. What changes is the body, the hold, and what you do with the space.

Where it started

Dominican

The original, from the Dominican Republic. Danced close but loose, with lots of free footwork and playful improvisation. Footwork-forward — expect taps, syncopations and quick changes, especially when the music goes into a mambo. Tends to be bouncier and more relaxed than the styles that grew out of it.

Body-led

Sensual

Developed in Spain in the 2000s. Much closer hold, far less footwork, and the movement comes from the body — waves, isolations, circular motion through the ribcage and hips. Heavily partner-connected, so it depends on lead and follow rather than individual footwork.

Turn-heavy

Moderna / Urban

Bridges the two. Keeps the Dominican footwork base but adds turn patterns, open position work, and figures borrowed from salsa and urban dance. This is what's most commonly taught in social classes outside the DR.

Old-school

Traditional / Bachatango and other fusions

Traditional bachata is the closed-hold, side-to-side social dancing the music was written for — no turns, just connection and musicality. Fusions like bachatango graft moves from other dances on top; they're fun, but learn a base style first.

Which to learn first is mostly about your local scene — dance whichever one the people around you are dancing. The basic step is the same either way, which is why it's worth drilling before you pick a lane.
